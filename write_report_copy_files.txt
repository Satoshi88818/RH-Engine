## 1. Two different things called "repulsion"

- **The Coulomb law of the heat flow**, ż = 2 Σ 1/(z_k − z_j), is exact, but it describes how zeros move in the fictitious flow time t. It is not a force the zeros exert on each other at t = 0.
- **Statistical repulsion at t = 0** is the observed fact that nearby zeros avoid each other: the density of spacings goes like s², as for eigenvalues of random unitary matrices. This is what this report examines.

## 2. The first link: the explicit formula, measured

For a smooth window w around height T₀, the explicit formula gives

> S(u) = Σ_γ w(γ − T₀) e^{{iγu}} ≈ −(e^{{−iT₀ log n}}/2π) · Λ(n)/√n · ŵ(u − log n)

near each u = log n. I computed S(log n) directly from the zero lists and compared it with Λ(n)/(2π√n) · ∫w. The ratio S/(−A) (after removing the known phase e^{{iT₀ log n}}):

So the zeros "know" the primes to about five digits, including the sign and phase, and nothing appears at non-prime-powers. (My zeros are located by the Riemann–Siegel sum, so I repeated the test on the independent published table.)

## 3. The ramp: K(τ) from primes, and saturation

Define K(τ) = ⟨|S(u)|²⟩ / Σw², with u = τ ln(γ/2π) (so τ = 1 is one mean spacing). Averaging the explicit formula over a band of u gives

> K_prime(τ) = (1/(L·B)) Σ_{{log n in band}} Λ(n)² / n,  with L = ln(γ/2π), B = band width,

which is τ asymptotically, since Σ_{{n ≤ x}} Λ(n)²/n ≈ ½ ln²x. I computed this from all primes up to 2×10⁸ (11.1 million), and K(τ) from the zeros by a non-uniform FFT, in bins of width 0.02.

- **For τ < 1**, K from the zeros equals the prime-power sum to within the statistical error at every height (figure, upper right). At low τ they agree to three digits because the band then contains only a few individual peaks; this is Parseval, not luck.
- **For τ > 1** the prime sum would keep growing like τ. The zeros' K does not: its mean over 1.3 < τ < 1.9 is {', '.join(f"{sat[n][0]:.3f}±{sat[n][1]:.3f}" for n in sat)} at heights 10⁷, 10⁸, 10⁹, 10¹⁰, 10¹¹, i.e. saturated at 1. **I observe this saturation; I cannot derive it from the primes.** It is Montgomery's conjecture F(α) = 1 for α ≥ 1.

## 4. From the ramp to the repulsion

The Fourier relation between K(τ) and the pair correlation R₂(s) gives, for small s,

> R₂(s) ≈ c₂ s², c₂ = 4π² ∫₀^∞ τ² (1 − K(τ)) dτ.

For K = min(τ, 1) this is π²/3, the random-matrix value: a ramp up to 1 and flat beyond is exactly what produces the s² repulsion. Using my **measured** K(τ) (with K = 1 beyond τ = 2) to predict the repulsion strength relative to random matrices, R_K = 12 ∫ τ²(1 − K) dτ:

It comes from structure near and above τ = 1, which is the subject of §6 and §7.

## 5. The second link: finite-height corrections, tested without free parameters

> Λ = γ₀² + 2γ₁ + Σ_p ln²p/(p−1)², C = Σ_p ln³p/(p−1)² / Λ.

## 6. The arithmetic behind the correction: lines at τ = 1 + ln q / L

In the Bogomolny–Keating formula the off-diagonal part is (1/4π²) |ζ(1+iε)|² e^{{i2πρε}} ∏_p [1 − (1−p^{{iε}})²/(p−1)²] + c.c. Expanding the product in p^{{iε}} turns it into a sum of exponentials e^{{iε ln r}} over rationals r, each of which becomes a **narrow line in K(τ) at τ = 1 + ln r / L with weight g(r)/L²**. I computed g(r) from the Euler product (e.g. g(2) = 2.0, g(3) = 0.75, g(6) = 1.5, g(10) = 0.625) and looked for the lines in the zeros.

- **Positions match.** The strongest excesses sit exactly at q = 2, 6, 3, 10 (figure, lower left; for γ ≈ 10⁹: τ = 1.037, 1.095, 1.058, 1.122, predicted 1.0367, 1.0948, 1.0581, 1.1219).
- **Weights scale like 1/L².** The weight times L² (squarefree q, integrated over ±0.06 in u):

A check with different stretch lengths and baselines moved the q = 2 value between 1.2 and 1.8, so my estimator has roughly ±20% scatter. **I could not tell whether the remaining shortfall is estimator bias or a genuine finite-L correction.** The published set below 1.13×10⁶ is not used here: its heights span a decade, which smears the lines.

- The sum of weights of these lines is not the same as the single-spike weight Λ/L² that BBLM use: their first-order treatment replaces all the lines by one effective peak at τ = α (the centroid of the lines), which is why N_eff and α summarise the effect.

## 7. Status of the "mechanism"

| step | status |
|---|---|
| zeros ↔ primes (explicit formula) | exact; verified numerically (§2) |
| K(τ) = τ for τ < 1 from Σ Λ(n)²/n | Montgomery's theorem under RH (restricted test functions); verified numerically (§3) |
| K = 1 for τ > 1 | observed (§3); conjectural (Montgomery) |
| ramp ⇒ s² repulsion | exact Fourier relation (§4) |
| finite-height weakening | derived from the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture plus a "kernel-change" conjecture; **passes a parameter-free test** (§5); line structure observed (§6) |

So the repulsion is, in a precise sense, the Fourier dual of the way the primes' contributions to the zeros grow with frequency: linearly up to one mean spacing, and then not at all. Why they stop growing at τ = 1 is the open part.

## 8. Limitations

- Floating-point numerics; zero lists from my own code and from a GitHub mirror of Odlyzko's tables (sanitised, numbers unchanged per its author).
- The finite-height theory is conjectural, and N_eff < 3 is outside its reliable range.
- The R_K check has noise of about ±0.05; it supports consistency, not precision.
- Line weights are only roughly measured (25–30% below prediction, ±20% scatter).
- Nothing here bears on whether any zero is off the critical line.

## 9. Next steps

- Test the three-point and higher correlations, which the same prime-pair formula predicts.
- Push K(τ) above τ = 1 with independent zero samples at many heights to resolve the lines individually.
