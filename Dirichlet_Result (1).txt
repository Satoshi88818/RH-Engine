# Do the zeta results carry over to other L-functions?

## Summary

I computed the zeros of four real Dirichlet L-functions, L(s, χ) for χ₋₃, χ₋₄, χ₅ and χ₈, up to height t = 700 (2,344 zeros in total: 568, 646, 537 and 593, each matching the expected count to within one). Three things carry over, and one does not.

1. **The explicit-formula peaks carry over, with the character.** The Fourier transform of the zeros has peaks at u = log n of height χ(n)Λ(n)/(2π√n), with the sign of χ(n): the measured ratio is 0.9995–1.0001 for all 61 prime powers with χ(n) ≠ 0. At prime powers with χ(n) = 0 (powers of 2 for q = 4, 8; of 3 for q = 3; of 5 for q = 5) there is no peak (|S| ≤ 0.04 against a typical peak of 30).
2. **The ramp carries over.** The form factor equals the prime-power sum ΣΛ(n)²χ(n)²/n for τ < 0.95 (for example 0.04 / 0.04, 0.40 / 0.40, 0.37 / 0.37, 0.50 / 0.49 for χ₋₃), and then stops growing: its mean over 1.25 < τ < 1.7 is 0.96–1.13 for the four characters, against a prime sum that would reach 1.65.
3. **The strong low-height deficit of close pairs carries over.** At ln(qt/2π) ≈ 5.2–6.1 the number of gaps with ν < 0.5 is 0.57 of the random-matrix count (141 against 247, z = −6.7).
4. **The zeta formula does not carry over unchanged.** The full zeta BK function, evaluated at the same ln(qt/2π), predicts 180 close pairs (ν < 0.5), and the data have 141 (z = −2.9); all four characters lie below it (ratios 0.65–0.88). The zeta formula explains about 63% of the deficit. A control shows this is not a failure of the formula at low height: for the zeta zeros at ln(γ/2π) = 5.4–5.9 the same formula predicts 143.5 and the observed count is 147 (z = +0.3). So the character changes the finite-height correction, as the arithmetic (the Euler product and the prime-pair term) would suggest.

This is exploratory: the samples are small (about 2,200 gaps, heights below 700), and I did not derive the formula adapted to characters.

## 1. Method

For real primitive χ of conductor q with parity a (a = 0 even, 1 odd), Λ(1/2 + it) = (q/π)^{(1/2+it+a)/2} Γ((1/2+it+a)/2) L(1/2+it, χ) is real, so the zeros are the sign changes of Z(t) = Re(e^{iθ(t)} L(1/2+it, χ)), with θ the phase of the prefactor. I evaluated L through Hurwitz zeta functions (`mpmath.dirichlet`) on a grid of step 0.07 from t = 1 to 700, found zeros as sign changes with a cubic-spline root finder, and re-sampled finely wherever two sign changes were adjacent or a near-miss extremum suggested a close pair (one region in total).

**Checks.** The imaginary part of the rotated L is 10⁻¹² or smaller (so Z really is real), and the zero counts match the smooth count (T/2π) ln(qT/2πe): 568 / 568, 646 / 645, 537 / 536, 593 / 593.

**Normalisation.** The mean density at height t is ln(qt/2π)/2π, so ν = gap × ln(qt/2π)/2π and L = ln(qt/2π). I used gaps with midpoint t ≥ 80.

## 3. Reading the result

- The universal parts (that the zeros contain the primes, with their character, and that the form factor ramps along the prime sum and then saturates) are robust across these four L-functions.
- The finite-height correction depends on the L-function. For characters the diagonal part changes through the weights |χ(p)|² (primes dividing q drop out), and the off-diagonal prime-pair term changes because the Hardy–Littlewood singular series is twisted by χ (for χ₋₄, for example, the sign of the correlation of χ(n) and χ(n+h) flips between h ≡ 0 and h ≡ 2 mod 4). My zeta formula contains none of this, and the data lie below it.
- Adapting the formula would mean deriving the twisted singular series and the modified Euler product for each character. I did not do this, so I cannot say whether the adapted formula would close the remaining gap.

## 4. Limitations

- Small sample (about 2,200 gaps), low heights (t ≤ 700, ln(qt/2π) = 3.6–6.3), only real characters of conductor ≤ 8.
- The pooled z-score of −2.9 for ν < 0.5 is not large; ν < 0.3 gives −1.5. The thresholds are nested, so the z-scores are not independent.
- Hurwitz-zeta evaluation is slow (25–80 ms per point), which limits the height. A Riemann–Siegel-type formula for L(s, χ) would allow far larger samples.
- Nothing here bears on RH.

## 5. Next steps

- Derive the twisted Hardy–Littlewood singular series for a quadratic character and build the adapted BK function.
- Extend to complex characters (where the off-diagonal term is suppressed by about 1/q) and to larger conductors, where the prediction is simpler and the test sharper.
- Compute zeros with a fast formula to heights of 10⁵ and beyond.
