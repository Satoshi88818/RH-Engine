# How primes and zeros are linked, and where the repulsion comes from

## Summary

1. **The link is exact and I measured it.** In the Fourier transform of the zeros, every prime power n produces a peak at u = log n with height Λ(n)/(2π√n) (times the window integral). The measured peaks match for all 267 prime powers n ≤ 1500: ratio 1.0000 near γ = 10⁹ and 1.0001 near 10¹¹, and also 1.0000 for **Odlyzko's published zeros** (1.86 million zeros, γ from 10⁵ to 1.13×10⁶), which are independent of my code.
2. **The statistical repulsion has an arithmetic source for τ < 1.** The zeros' form factor K(τ) is the prime-power sum Σ Λ(n)²/n up to τ = 1 (it ramps up linearly), and then saturates at 1. The ramp, through the Fourier relation between the form factor and the pair correlation, is what makes the zeros repel like random-matrix eigenvalues (gap density ∝ s²).
3. **The finite-height weakening of the repulsion is explained by primes, with no fitted parameters.** Bogomolny et al.'s correction (constants Λ and C computed from the primes, reproduced here to 5 digits) removes most of the discrepancy: for N_eff ≥ 3 the rms z-score of the close-pair counts drops from 5.1 (random-matrix prediction) to 1.0.
4. **I also found the arithmetic behind that correction directly:** narrow lines in K(τ) at τ = 1 + ln q / ln(γ/2π) for squarefree q (q = 2, 3, 6, 10, …), whose positions match to within my bin width and whose weights, for the three cleanest lines (q = 2, 6, 10), are about 0.7–0.8 of the Hardy–Littlewood-based prediction (q = 3 is about 0.5).
5. **What is not established.** The saturation K = 1 beyond τ = 1, which the strength of the repulsion depends on, is observed here and conjectured by Montgomery, but not derived from primes. The finite-height theory rests on the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture and one further conjecture (§5). So this explains how primes shape the zeros' statistics; it does not derive the repulsion from first principles, and it does not bear on RH.

## 1. Two different things called "repulsion"

- **The Coulomb law of the heat flow**, ż = 2 Σ 1/(z_k − z_j), is exact, but it describes how zeros move in the fictitious flow time t. It is not a force the zeros exert on each other at t = 0.
- **Statistical repulsion at t = 0** is the observed fact that nearby zeros avoid each other: the density of spacings goes like s², as for eigenvalues of random unitary matrices. This is what this report examines.

## 2. The first link: the explicit formula, measured

For a smooth window w around height T₀, the explicit formula gives

> S(u) = Σ_γ w(γ − T₀) e^{iγu} ≈ −(e^{−iT₀ log n}/2π) · Λ(n)/√n · ŵ(u − log n)

near each u = log n. I computed S(log n) directly from the zero lists and compared it with Λ(n)/(2π√n) · ∫w. The ratio S/(−A) (after removing the known phase e^{iT₀ log n}):

| zero set | zeros | prime powers tested | mean ratio | std | rms of S at non-prime-powers vs typical prime peak |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mine, γ ≈ 10⁹ | 500,002 | 267 | 1.00003 | 0.00001 | 0.0382 vs 3.23e+03 |
| mine, γ ≈ 10¹¹ | 150,106 | 267 | 1.00006 | 0.00008 | 0.47 vs 781 |
| **Odlyzko, published** | 1,858,185 | 267 | 1.00000 | 0.00000 | 0.000662 vs 2e+04 |

So the zeros "know" the primes to about five digits, including the sign and phase, and nothing appears at non-prime-powers. (My zeros are located by the Riemann–Siegel sum, so I repeated the test on the independent published table.)

## 3. The ramp: K(τ) from primes, and saturation

Define K(τ) = ⟨|S(u)|²⟩ / Σw², with u = τ ln(γ/2π) (so τ = 1 is one mean spacing). Averaging the explicit formula over a band of u gives

> K_prime(τ) = (1/(L·B)) Σ_{log n in band} Λ(n)² / n,  with L = ln(γ/2π), B = band width,

which is τ asymptotically, since Σ_{n ≤ x} Λ(n)²/n ≈ ½ ln²x. I computed this from all primes up to 2×10⁸ (11.1 million), and K(τ) from the zeros by a non-uniform FFT, in bins of width 0.02.

- **For τ < 1**, K from the zeros equals the prime-power sum within the statistical error at every height (figure, upper right). Over 0.3 < τ < 0.95 the mean difference is below 0.002 and the rms difference is 0.005–0.010 (statistical error per bin 0.02–0.07), with no bin deviating by more than 2σ at heights 10⁷ to 10¹¹. At low τ the two agree to three digits because each band then contains only a few individual peaks; this is Parseval, not luck.
- **For τ > 1** the prime sum would keep growing like τ. The zeros' K does not: its mean over 1.3 < τ < 1.9 is 1.003±0.003, 0.997±0.003, 1.001±0.003, 0.999±0.004, 0.997±0.005 at heights 10⁷, 10⁸, 10⁹, 10¹⁰, 10¹¹, i.e. saturated at 1. **I observe this saturation; I cannot derive it from the primes.** It is Montgomery's conjecture F(α) = 1 for α ≥ 1.

## 4. From the ramp to the repulsion

The Fourier relation between K(τ) and the pair correlation R₂(s) gives, for small s,

> R₂(s) ≈ c₂ s², c₂ = 4π² ∫₀^∞ τ² (1 − K(τ)) dτ.

For K = min(τ, 1) this is π²/3, the random-matrix value: a ramp up to 1 and flat beyond is exactly what produces the s² repulsion. Using my **measured** K(τ) (with K = 1 beyond τ = 2) to predict the repulsion strength relative to random matrices, R_K = 12 ∫ τ²(1 − K) dτ:

| height | R_K from measured K | observed small-gap ratio (x = 0.2) | BBLM prediction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10⁷ | 0.878 | 0.911 | 0.895 |
| 10⁸ | 0.945 | 0.905 | 0.924 |
| 10⁹ | 0.927 | 0.958 | 0.942 |
| 10¹⁰ | 1.013 | 0.983 | 0.955 |
| 10¹¹ | 0.907 | 0.920 | 0.964 |

The two columns agree within the noise (about ±0.05 for R_K, ±0.02–0.04 for the ratios); their means are 0.934 and 0.935. **If instead only the diagonal prime sum is used, with K = 1 for τ ≥ 1, the predicted ratio is 1.01, 1.01, 1.01, 1.08, 1.21: no deficit at all.** So the weakening of the repulsion at finite height does not come from the ramp. It comes from structure near and above τ = 1, which is the subject of §6 and §7.

## 5. The second link: finite-height corrections, tested without free parameters

Bogomolny, Bohigas, Leboeuf and Monastra (2006) derived, from the Bogomolny–Keating two-point formula (which uses the Hardy–Littlewood prime-pair conjecture), that at height E the zeros behave like a CUE matrix of effective size N_eff = ln(E/2π)/√(12Λ), with the spacing variable rescaled by α = 1 + C/ln(E/2π), where

> Λ = γ₀² + 2γ₁ + Σ_p ln²p/(p−1)², C = Σ_p ln³p/(p−1)² / Λ.

I computed these from the primes (to 2×10⁸, with a tail estimate): **Λ = 1.57315** (paper: 1.57314…) and **C = 1.4721** (paper: 1.4720). I then computed the predicted nearest-neighbour CDF F(x) = F₀(x) + G₁(αx) / (α N_eff²) numerically (Fredholm determinants for the sine kernel and for CUE_N, N = 60 and 120 extrapolated) and compared the number of gaps with normalised size ν below x with the random-matrix prediction and with this one, using each gap's own height.

- For **N_eff < 3** (the published zeros below 1.13×10⁶) the correction takes the z-scores from 15.5 to 4.7: much better, but visibly over-corrected at larger x (up to +11.7 at x = 0.5). This is expected, because the expansion is in 1/N_eff² and N_eff is only 1.5–2.8 there.
- At γ ≈ 10²⁰ and 10²¹ both predictions are within noise (the correction is about 1%).
- z-scores treat the counts as Poisson; neighbouring gaps are correlated, so the z-scores are somewhat optimistic.

## 6. The arithmetic behind the correction: lines at τ = 1 + ln q / L

In the Bogomolny–Keating formula the off-diagonal part is (1/4π²) |ζ(1+iε)|² e^{i2πρε} ∏_p [1 − (1−p^{iε})²/(p−1)²] + c.c. Expanding the product in p^{iε} turns it into a sum of exponentials e^{iε ln r} over rationals r, each of which becomes a **narrow line in K(τ) at τ = 1 + ln r / L with weight g(r)/L²**. I computed g(r) from the Euler product (e.g. g(2) = 2.0, g(3) = 0.75, g(6) = 1.5, g(10) = 0.625) and looked for the lines in the zeros.

- **Positions match.** The strongest excesses sit exactly at q = 2, 6, 3, 10 (figure, lower left; for γ ≈ 10⁹ the strongest bins are centred at τ = 1.038, 1.094, 1.058, 1.122, and the predicted positions 1 + ln q / L are 1.0367, 1.0949, 1.0582, 1.1219, all within the 0.004 bin width).
- **Weights scale like 1/L².** The weight times L² (squarefree q, integrated over ±0.06 in u):

| height | ln(γ/2π) | q = 2 | q = 6 | q = 10 | q = 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1e7 | 14.3 | 1.63 | 1.16 | 0.38 | 0.35 |
| 1e8 | 16.6 | 1.64 | 1.06 | 0.44 | 0.37 |
| 1e9 | 18.9 | 1.38 | 1.08 | 0.42 | 0.45 |
| 1e10 | 21.2 | 1.59 | 1.26 | 0.45 | 0.28 |
| 1e11 | 23.5 | 1.36 | 1.21 | 0.44 | 0.36 |
| **mean** | | **1.52** | **1.15** | **0.43** | **0.36** |
| **predicted g(q)** | | **2.00** | **1.50** | **0.63** | **0.75** |

So the measured line weights are about 0.76 (q = 2), 0.77 (q = 6), 0.69 (q = 10) and 0.48 (q = 3) of the prediction. A check with different stretch lengths and baselines moved the q = 2 value between 1.2 and 1.8, so my estimator has roughly ±20% scatter. **I could not tell whether the remaining shortfall is estimator bias or a genuine finite-L correction.** The published set below 1.13×10⁶ is not used here: its heights span a decade, which smears the lines.

- The sum of weights of these lines is not the same as the single-spike weight Λ/L² that BBLM use: their first-order treatment replaces all the lines by one effective peak at τ = α (the centroid of the lines), which is why N_eff and α summarise the effect.

## 7. Status of the "mechanism"

| step | status |
|---|---|
| zeros ↔ primes (explicit formula) | exact; verified numerically (§2) |
| K(τ) = τ for τ < 1 from Σ Λ(n)²/n | Montgomery's theorem under RH (restricted test functions); verified numerically (§3) |
| K = 1 for τ > 1 | observed (§3); conjectural (Montgomery) |
| ramp ⇒ s² repulsion | exact Fourier relation (§4) |
| finite-height weakening | derived from the Hardy–Littlewood conjecture plus a "kernel-change" conjecture; **passes a parameter-free test** (§5); line structure observed (§6) |

So the repulsion is, in a precise sense, the Fourier dual of the way the primes' contributions to the zeros grow with frequency: linearly up to one mean spacing, and then not at all. Why they stop growing at τ = 1 is the open part.

## 8. Limitations

- Floating-point numerics; zero lists from my own code and from a GitHub mirror of Odlyzko's tables (sanitised, numbers unchanged per its author).
- The finite-height theory is conjectural, and N_eff < 3 is outside its reliable range.
- The R_K check has noise of about ±0.05; it supports consistency, not precision.
- Line weights are only roughly measured (25–30% below the prediction for q = 2, 6, 10 and about half for q = 3, with about ±20% scatter in my estimator).
- Nothing here bears on whether any zero is off the critical line.
